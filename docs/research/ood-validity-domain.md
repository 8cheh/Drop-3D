# Out-of-Distribution / Validity-Domain Rejection for a Physics-Based Pendant-Drop Tensiometry Pipeline

**Technical research report.** Scope: how to make an axisymmetric Young–Laplace (ADSA) pendant-drop pipeline *refuse to answer* when the input is outside the domain where its assumptions hold — with both the physics side (which is the primary, load-bearing side) and the ML side (in case a learned component is added later).

## 1. Framing the problem

### 1.1 Your five failure modes, classified

The crucial conceptual point first: **"OOD detection" as the ML literature defines it is a poor match for your problem, and the literature that actually matches is the *applicability domain* / *validity domain* literature from QSAR, chemometrics and analytical chemistry.** [SYN, but grounded — see §1.2.] The difference is not cosmetic:

| # | Failure mode | What it really is | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | Input is not a drop at all | **Classic OOD / novelty detection** | The image is off the data manifold entirely. This *is* the ML OOD problem. |
| (b) | Drop present, segmented wrongly | **Pre-processing / measurement-instrument fault** — a *garbage-in* gate, not OOD | The physics model is fine; the *observation* is corrupt. Detected by edge-detection consistency checks, not by distributional distance. |
| (c) | Geometry violated (not axisymmetric, tilted, non-static) | **Model-assumption validity** (a *mechanistic* applicability-domain check) | Your forward model assumes axisymmetry + hydrostatic equilibrium. The input may be perfectly in-distribution statistically while the *assumption* is false. No density model will catch this. |
| (d) | Physically valid but ill-conditioned | **Applicability domain in the *response/parameter* space** — the classic QSAR "extrapolation" case | The fit is fine; the *inverse problem* has no information. This is the case the QSAR/chemometrics term was invented for. |
| (e) | Fit did not converge / wrong branch | **Identifiability / non-uniqueness** — a property of the inverse problem, not of the data distribution | Kratz & Kierfeld map exactly where multiple shape branches exist (see §2.3). |

**[S]** That (c) and (d) are distinct from (a) is the central organising idea in the QSAR/applicability-domain literature: the applicability domain (AD) is defined as the *response- and descriptor-space region in which the model's predictions can be considered reliable*, and "query chemicals that satisfy the scope of the model are considered as within the AD and classified as interpolated whereas the rest are extrapolations and thus, outside the AD" ([Sahigara et al., *Molecules* 17(5):4791–4810, 2012, PMC6268288](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)). Note that this definition is explicitly about **reliable**, not about **typical**.

### 1.2 Standard terminology and the canonical review papers

**[S]** The term-of-art is **Applicability Domain (AD)**, occasionally **validity domain** or **domain of applicability**. The trigger for the whole field was a regulatory requirement, not a statistical one: the OECD principles for (Q)SAR validation require "a defined domain of applicability" as one of five validation principles, and REACH permits QSAR predictions only if validity is justified ([Sahigara et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)).

### 1.3 The structural implication for your architecture

**[SYN]** Because (a)–(e) are five *different* problems, a single scalar "OOD score" with one threshold is the wrong design. The right design is a **layered cascade of independent gates, each with its own diagnostic, its own threshold, and its own named error code**, terminating in an "accept / report-with-caveat / refuse" verdict. This is exactly ADAN's count-of-unmet-criteria logic ([Carrió et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821140/)) applied to physics diagnostics. §5 gives a concrete version.

## 2. Physics-based validity checks

### 2.1 Residual-based checks

**What good vs bad residuals look like.** [SYN, but standard nonlinear-regression theory.] For a correct model, correct axisymmetry, correct edge detection, and errors dominated by ~i.i.d. edge-localisation noise of scale σ, the residual vector r ∈ R^N (N ≈ number of profile points, typically 200–2000) should be approximately **i.i.d. N(0, σ²)**. The characteristic *bad* signatures are:

| Residual signature | Physical cause |
|---|---|
| Uniformly inflated amplitude, no structure | Segmentation noise too large; blur; insufficient resolution. |
| Smooth, low-order systematic curve | Wrong branch / wrong Bond number / wrong density difference Δρ; incorrect pixel scale. |
| **Left–right antisymmetric pattern** (odd function of horizontal coordinate) | Tilt, non-axisymmetry, camera roll, or a wrongly-located symmetry axis. |
| **Persistent one-sided residual** (one side of the profile consistently inside/outside the fit) | Illumination gradient, refractive-index distortion, or a real asymmetry. |
| High-frequency, spatially correlated along arc length | Aliasing in edge detection; sub-pixel interpolation artefacts. |
| Amplitude exploding at the drop apex or at the needle | Edge detection failure at high-curvature or occluded regions. |
| **Oscillatory / sign-alternating with runs structure** | Over-fitting to noise (too many free parameters) or a genuinely wrong functional form. |

**Which statistical tests.** All of the following are standard and all are essentially free to compute. **[S]** They are documented in the NIST/SEMATECH *e-Handbook of Statistical Methods*, which is the best freely-available reference for exactly these tests (I verified the handbook is live at [https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/); the specific test pages are at `eda/section3/eda35.htm` (normality tests incl. Anderson–Darling, Shapiro–Wilk, and the skewness/kurtosis chi-square test) and `pmc/section4/pmc44.htm` (runs test and autocorrelation) — **[UNVERIFIED specific page URLs: the handbook's internal URLs shifted when the site was re-platformed; I could not retrieve those two pages during this research. The tests themselves are textbook-standard.]**

1. **Reduced chi-square / chi-square goodness of fit.**
   χ²_red = Σ rᵢ²/(σᵢ² ν), with ν = N − p (p = number of fitted parameters: for ADSA typically 4–5 — γ or Δρ, apex radius R₀, apex position (x₀, z₀), and possibly needle/pixel scale). **Expected value is 1** if σ is the true per-point edge-detection uncertainty *and* the model is correct. Reject if χ²_red is far above 1. This test is only as good as your σ; see §5.2 for how to calibrate σ empirically.
2. **Residual *shape*, not just magnitude — the most informative single diagnostic.** [SYN] Fit the residual vector as a function of arc length s to a low-order polynomial basis and a symmetry basis. Report (i) the amplitude of the antisymmetric component and (ii) the amplitude of the low-order polynomial component, each normalised by σ. These two scalars separate {tilt/asymmetry} from {wrong branch/scale}, which χ²_red alone cannot.
3. **Runs test / Wald–Wolfowitz.** Count sign runs in the ordered residual sequence. For i.i.d. residuals the number of runs R is approximately normal with mean 2N₊N₋/N + 1 and a known variance; **too few runs ⇒ systematic structure; too many ⇒ over-fitting/oscillation**. [S] Standard formulation in the NIST handbook (§`pmc/section4`). [UNVERIFIED page URL.]
4. **Lag-1 autocorrelation of residuals** (equivalently, **Durbin–Watson**). χ²_red and the runs test are to a degree redundant with autocorrelation; the lag-1 autocorrelation coefficient is the cheapest and most interpretable. [S] Same handbook reference. [UNVERIFIED page URL.] [SYN] For a pendant profile, compute the autocorrelation along **arc length**, not along the raw row/column index — otherwise you will get spurious structure from the profile's geometry.
5. **Normality tests** (Shapiro–Wilk for N ≲ 5000, Anderson–Darling, or Jarque–Bera). [S] NIST handbook §`eda/section3/eda35.htm`. [UNVERIFIED page URL.] [SYN] Caveat: with N = 1000+ profile points, *any* normality test will reject at conventional α because the test's power grows with N while real edge-detection errors have slightly heavier tails. **Do not use normality as a hard gate.** Use it as a *soft* score, and prefer a robust scale estimate (MAD) over the standard deviation for thresholding.
6. **Leverage / influence.** [S] The leverage matrix **H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ** has diagonal entries that are "proportional to Hotelling's T² statistic and Mahalanobis distance measure", and "can be applied only on the regression models. The approach can be associated with a **warning leverage, generally three times the average of the leverage that corresponds to p/n** where p is the number of model parameters while n is the number of training compounds" ([Sahigara et al. 2012, §2.2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)). [SYN] Applied *per profile point*, with p ≈ 5 and n ≈ 1000, this gives a warning leverage around 0.015 — too tight to be useful pointwise. **Apply leverage at the image level instead**: build X from a small feature vector per accepted measurement (e.g. log Bo, Wo, P_s, R₀ in pixels, profile point count, residual amplitude, asymmetry amplitude, mean edge gradient) over a library of n accepted runs; then warning leverage = 3p/n. With p = 8 and n = 300 accepted runs, that is **0.08** [SYN]. This is one of the highest value-per-line-of-code checks available.
7. **Distance to the detachment/bifurcation boundary** — see §2.3; this is a bounded, physically-interpretable conditioning measure and is far more informative than any generic multivariate distance.

**Reference for the regression-diagnostics theory itself:** R. D. Cook & S. Weisberg, *Residuals and Influence in Regression* (1982) and Bates & Watts, *Nonlinear Regression Analysis and Its Applications* (1988) are the canonical texts. **[UNVERIFIED: I did not retrieve these during this research; I am citing them from background knowledge as the standard references — verify before citing formally.]**

### 2.2 Axisymmetry tests from a single view

This is a genuine and important limitation: **a single view cannot prove axisymmetry.** It can only *falsify* it, and only for asymmetries that project onto the image plane. Be explicit about this in your report — claiming "we verify axisymmetry" from one camera would be over-claiming.

**What you can do from one view** [SYN, assembled from standard ADSA practice]:

1. **Left–right profile symmetry residual.** After fitting, reflect the extracted profile about the fitted symmetry axis x = x₀ and compute the pointwise discrepancy between the left and right branches as a function of height z. Report the RMS of (x_left(z) − 2x₀ + x_right(z)) and, more informatively, its **shape** (constant offset ⇒ x₀ mis-estimate; linear-in-z ⇒ tilt/roll; higher order ⇒ genuine non-axisymmetry or optical distortion).
2. **Compare separate left-only and right-only fits.** Fit the Young–Laplace solution to the left branch alone and to the right branch alone. Report Δγ/γ between them, and Δ(apex radius), Δ(Δρ). **[S/YN]** This is a real technique in the drop-shape literature — the general idea of *scrutinising the profile and the fit for internal consistency* is the core of Hoorfar & Neumann's systematic audit of ADSA hardware and software ([Hoorfar & Neumann, "Recent progress in axisymmetric drop shape analysis (ADSA)", *Adv Colloid Interface Sci* 121(1–3):25–49, 2006, PMID 16854362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16854362/)), which documents that the technique has "convergence problems for nearly spherical drops" due to "numerical truncation and accumulation of round-off errors". [SYN] I could not find a paper that specifically validates the "split-half fit" test for axisymmetry; I believe it is sound but it should be presented as your own diagnostic, calibrated against synthetic non-axisymmetric shapes (which you can generate) rather than presented as a cited method.
3. **Tilt detection via the apex normal.** For an axisymmetric drop in hydrostatic equilibrium, the profile normal at the apex is vertical. Fit the apex tangent; the angle between it and the image vertical is a direct tilt diagnostic. **[S]** The Young–Laplace/hydrostatic balance that underpins this is standard ADSA theory (Saad & Neumann, "Axisymmetric Drop Shape Analysis (ADSA): An Outline", *Adv Colloid Interface Sci* 238:62–87, 2016, PMID [27855787](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27855787/)).
4. **Dynamic/non-static detection (for your rolling-droplet work).** [SYN, this is the weakest-sourced part of the report — flag it.] If you take *n* frames: (i) residual rise with frame-to-frame profile displacement; (ii) the fitted γ drifting monotonically within a burst; (iii) profile-shape change exceeding the static model's residual floor. The cleanest formal test is a **residual-based change detector across frames**: for each frame compute χ²_red and the residual shape features, then test whether the sequence is consistent with a single static population. **[S]** The physical reason the static assumption breaks is well documented for interfaces with adsorbed layers: "traditional shape-fitting techniques — which assume an isotropic and spatially homogeneous stress — are inapplicable for complex interfaces", and applying ADSA to such interfaces leads to "incorrect predictions of the interfacial properties" ([Rodríguez-Hakim, Jaensson & Vermant, *Rheologica Acta* 64:461–481, 2025, doi 10.1007/s00397-025-01493-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00397-025-01493-z), CC BY, open access). **That paper is highly relevant to your dynamic rolling-droplet extension and I recommend reading it in full.**

### 2.3 Conditioning-based checks — a-priori indicators and published thresholds

This is where the pendant-drop literature is richest, and where the best-sourced thresholds live.

**The dimensionless numbers.** [S] Using the notation of [Kratz & Kierfeld, "Pendant Drop Tensiometry: A Machine Learning Approach", *J. Chem. Phys.* 153, 094102 (2020), preprint free at arXiv:2006.10111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10111):

- **Worthington number** `Wo ≡ Δρ̃ · Ṽ/π = Δρ g V / (π γ a)`, where a is the capillary length. "This parameter measures the **distance to the detachment volume** according to Tate's law (17) such that Wo < 1 is bounded and **Wo ≃ 1 corresponds to a droplet close to detachment**, while Wo ≪ 1 corresponds to droplets far from detachment." **[S]** (Kratz & Kierfeld, Eq. 18 and surrounding text.)
- **Bond number** `Bo` — "the bond number Bo is the only shape control parameter" for a pendant drop at fixed geometry. **[S]** (Kratz & Kierfeld, §II.)
- **Shape parameter `P_s`** — a differential-geometry-based scalar introduced specifically *to quantify the meaning of "well-deformed" drops and "close to spherical" drops* and thereby to **"provide an a priori knowledge of the accuracy of the results"**. **[S]** (Hoorfar & Neumann 2006 abstract, PMID [16854362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16854362/); see also Hoorfar et al., *Langmuir* 22(1):52–56, 2006, PMID [16378399](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16378399/), which presents "the surface tension measurement ... as a function of a shape parameter, P(s), which has been used to quantify the range of the applicability of ADSA".) **This is literally an applicability-domain criterion published in 2006 for your exact method. Use the term.**
- **Neumann number `Ne ≡ (Δρ g R₀ H)/γ`** — **[S]** proposed as a *replacement* for the classical Bond number: "the classical Bond number failed to predict the accuracy of drop shape analysis at very small drop volumes. Thus, we proposed a replacement of the classical Bond number, called the Neumann number Ne ... The design rationale ... lies in the use of the geometric mean of the radius of curvature at the drop apex (R₀) and the drop height (H) as the new characteristic length (L) ... It is found that the Neumann number is capable of evaluating the accuracy of ADSA." ([Yang, Yu & Zuo, *Langmuir* 33(36):8914–8923, 2017, PMID 28793193](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28793193/).) **[S]** They also propose a **local** Neumann number `Ne_z ≡ (Δρ g R₀/γ)·z` "in evaluating the contribution of the local drop profile to the accuracy of ADSA" — i.e. a per-profile-region conditioning diagnostic. **This is directly implementable and I rank it highly.**

### 2.4 Physical plausibility ranges

**[S]** What is *firmly* established:
- `0 < Wo < 1` is bounded, `Wo ≈ 1` ⇒ near detachment. ([Kratz & Kierfeld, arXiv:2006.10111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10111).)
- `Wo ≥ 1/2` is the well-conditioned region for conventional shape fitting near the Ω = 2,3 bifurcation. (ibid.)
- Multiple shape branches (classes) exist in the shape diagram; the wrong-branch hazard is real and is a function of position in the (Δρ̃, Ṽ) / (p̃_L, Wo) plane. (ibid.) **[S]** Kratz & Kierfeld's whole training-set design is about sampling "the first shaded 'triangle' enclosed by the yellow and blue bifurcation lines in the shape diagram Fig. 4" and about avoiding detached/green regions — a *direct* demonstration that the reliable parameter region is a bounded, non-convex region of the control-parameter plane.
- ADSA accuracy "deteriorates upon the reduction of drop volume" and the classical Bond number "failed to predict the accuracy of drop shape analysis at very small drop volumes". ([Yang et al. 2017, PMID 28793193](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28793193/).) **[S]**
- For small, sub-hemispherical, **low-Bond-number** drops, **capillary pressure tensiometry (CPT)** rather than shape fitting is the appropriate method ("CPT ... relies on the formation of small, low Bond number (Bo) sub-hemispherical drops"; "This method proves to be precise as well, especially when dealing with low Bond number drops or bubbles (Bo ≪ 1)"). ([Rodríguez-Hakim et al. 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00397-025-01493-z).) **[S]** ⇒ **your OOD policy should include a "this regime is served by a different method" refusal code, which is a policy statement rather than a numerical threshold.** [SYN]
- **The Bond number is not a sufficient accuracy predictor** (Yang et al. 2017; Rodríguez-Hakim et al. 2025 both say Wo is better). **[S]** ⇒ do not build your gate on Bo alone.

## 3. Statistical / ML methods

### 3.1 Conformal prediction for regression

**What it gives you.** **[S]** "Conformal prediction is a user-friendly paradigm for creating statistically rigorous uncertainty sets/intervals for the predictions of such models. Critically, the sets are valid in a **distribution-free** sense: they possess explicit, **non-asymptotic** guarantees even without distributional assumptions or model assumptions. One can use conformal prediction with any pre-trained model ... to produce sets that are guaranteed to contain the ground truth with a user-specified probability, such as 90%." ([Angelopoulos & Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification", arXiv:2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511).) That paper also "describe[s] its extensions to complex machine learning tasks involving structured outputs, **distribution shift**, time-series, **outliers**, **models that abstain**, and more" — the last two are exactly your use case, and the paper ships Jupyter notebooks ([code](https://github.com/aangelopoulos/conformal-prediction)). **[S]**

**Split (inductive) conformal, mechanically.** [S] Standard construction: hold out a calibration set; compute nonconformity scores (e.g. absolute residuals |y − ŷ|) on it; set the interval half-width to the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of those scores. **Key property: it requires only exchangeability of calibration and test points, not correctness of the model.** This is why it survives model misspecification — including the misspecification of a physics fitter.

**Inductive vs transductive.** [SYN, standard.] **Transductive** (full) conformal prediction re-fits the model once per candidate test point and is therefore computationally infeasible for a nonlinear least-squares Young–Laplace fit. **Inductive** (split) conformal fits once and calibrates on a held-out set — this is the only variant you should consider. **[S]** Angelopoulos & Bates explicitly cover this distinction and recommend inductive/split conformal in practice.

**Mondrian conformal.** [SYN, standard.] Mondrian conformal prediction computes the calibration quantile **separately within each category** of a (possibly chosen) partition of the input space, restoring *conditional* (per-category) rather than merely marginal coverage. **[S]** A recent, directly analogous application: for neutron-star equation-of-state surrogates, "Across α ∈ [0.05, 0.25], empirical coverages closely track 1−α for both Standard and Mondrian CP; **in conservative regimes, Mondrian yields narrower average physical widths at comparable coverage**", and it is described as "the first application of class-conditioned (Mondrian) conformal calibration to neutron-star EoS surrogates" ([arXiv:2602.19363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19363)). **[S]** For you, the natural Mondrian categories are **Wo bins** (e.g. Wo < 0.1, 0.1–0.5, 0.5–1) — because the residual distribution is strongly Wo-dependent (§2.3). **[SYN on the specific choice; S on the technique.]**

**Using conformal to flag OOD by interval width.** This is the key mechanism for your problem [SYN, but the reasoning is standard]:
1. Calibrate a conformal interval for γ at level 1−α on a library of *known-good* measurements (reference liquids, or synthetic profiles with known γ plus realistic simulated edge noise).
2. For a new image, produce γ̂ and its conformal interval [γ̂ − q, γ̂ + q].
3. **Declare "out of domain" if the interval half-width q exceeds a physical tolerance** τ (e.g. 1 mN/m, or 2 % of γ̂) — this is exactly the "flag inputs whose prediction interval is unacceptably wide" pattern you asked about.
4. Additionally, **flag if the *base model's* point prediction is outside the calibrated score distribution's support in a way that breaks the exchangeability assumption.** Important honesty point: **conformal validity holds only under exchangeability.** If you calibrate on static axisymmetric drops and then feed it a rolling droplet, exchangeability is violated and the nominal coverage is no longer guaranteed. **[S]** This is precisely why the modern conformal literature has a whole sub-field on covariate shift — e.g. CoDrug weighs calibration samples by KDE-estimated density ratios and reports reducing "the coverage gap by over 35%" versus unadjusted conformal prediction on drug-property tasks under realistic drift ([arXiv:2310.12033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12033)); KMM-CP reports "reduc[ing] coverage gap by over 50%" on molecular property benchmarks ([arXiv:2603.26415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26415)). **[S]** MAPIE has added **exchangeability tests** explicitly "to help users verify when MAPIE can be legitimately applied" ([MAPIE docs](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)). **[S]**
5. **Therefore: conformal prediction is excellent for quantifying and refusing on *uncertainty width*, and useless on its own for detecting *assumption* violations.** [SYN] Pair it with a physics gate, always.

### 3.2 Density / distance-based OOD detection

All of these operate on a **feature vector per image**, not on pixels [SYN]:
**x** = (log Wo, log Bo, log P_s or Ne, R₀ in pixels, profile point count, χ²_red, asymmetry amplitude, low-order residual amplitude, frame-to-frame drift).

- **Mahalanobis distance.** [S] Handles correlated descriptors via the covariance matrix; its iso-distance contours are ellipsoids. "The unique feature associated with Mahalanobis measure is the co-variance matrix which can handle the correlated descriptors." Threshold strategies compared by Sahigara et al. include `maxdist`, `2d̄`, `3d̄`, `p95`, and `d̄ + z·std` (z = 0.5 default), **with the conclusion that "the third and fourth strategies ... the thresholds corresponding to twice the value of d and that corresponding to 95 percentile converged significantly"** ([Sahigara et al. 2012, §3.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)). **[S]** ⇒ **practical recommendation: use the 95th percentile of the training Mahalanobis distance as your threshold; it converged with the 2d̄ rule and is the better-founded of the two.** Caveat also in that paper: estimator instability when the number of features approaches the number of samples. [SYN] With n≈100–1000 accepted runs and 6–10 features you are comfortably fine, but use **Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage covariance** or a robust MCD estimate — plain sample covariance is corrupted by the very outliers you are trying to detect.
- **Leverage = Mahalanobis-equivalent** for regression, with the 3p/n warning rule. [S] Sahigara et al. 2012, as quoted in §2.1.
- **One-class SVM.** [S] Implemented in PyOD as `OCSVM` ([PyOD docs](https://pyod.readthedocs.io)); original method Schölkopf et al. 2001. [SYN] Needs a kernel bandwidth; more tuning than Mahalanobis for no clear benefit at your dimensionality.
- **Isolation forest.** [S] Implemented in PyOD as `IForest` ("Outlier Detection with 5 Lines of Code": `clf = IForest(); clf.fit(X_train)` — [PyPI PyOD](https://pypi.org/project/pyod/)). [SYN] Axis-aligned splits make it weak when your features are strongly correlated (Wo and Bo are); good for non-convex regions, which your operating window is.
- **Kernel density estimation.** [S] In the AD literature, "Probability Density Distribution-Based Method ... considered as one of the most advanced approaches"; the implementation is a Gaussian potential `p(xⱼ) = Σᵢ exp(−‖xᵢ−xⱼ‖²/2s²)` with a cutoff from a sample percentile, and "Test compounds with potential function values lower than this threshold are considered outside the AD." Its advantage: "their ability to identify the **internal empty regions**" ([Sahigara et al. 2012, §2.3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)). **[S]** This is important: your reliable region is plausibly non-convex (the Ω=2/Ω=3 branch structure), so a method that can represent internal holes is worth the extra effort.
- **kNN-based AD.** [S] The Sahigara 2013 method is explicitly designed to handle "asymmetric [distributions], with wide regions of low data density" and low sensitivity to the smoothing parameter k ([PMC3679843](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679843/)). **[S]** This is a very good fit to a pendant-drop operating window.
- **Autoencoder reconstruction error.** [S] Used for OOD detection in scientific ML — e.g. an SVGP-KAN framework explicitly demonstrating "out-of-distribution detection in convolutional autoencoders" ([arXiv:2512.05306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05306)). [SYN] **For your pipeline this is the wrong tool.** You already have a physically-principled forward model and a residual; an autoencoder's reconstruction error on the *image* mostly measures lighting/contrast/texture variation, not whether the physics assumptions hold. Use the Young–Laplace residual instead — it is an interpretable, physics-grounded reconstruction error with the same mathematical form and infinitely better semantics.

### 3.3 Uncertainty-based rejection

Which of these give useful signals **for a physics-based fitter**? [SYN — this ranking is my assessment, with sourced background on each method.]

| Method | Sourced background | Verdict for your pipeline |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaussian process predictive variance** | [S] Standard; GP regression gives a closed-form posterior predictive variance that grows away from training data. | **Useful, indirectly.** Do not put a GP on γ (you have physics for that). **Do** put a GP (or even a simple kernel-weighted estimator) on *your residuals as a function of the physics descriptors* — i.e. model `σ_residual(Wo, Bo, P_s)`. That gives you a *calibrated, input-dependent* uncertainty floor that grows in sparse regions. This is the highest-value ML component available to you, and it needs no OOD labels. |
| **Deep ensembles** | [S] "we evaluate the predictive uncertainty on test examples from known and unknown distributions, and show that our method is able to express higher uncertainty on out-of-distribution examples" ([Lakshminarayanan et al., arXiv:1612.01474](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)). | **Good if and only if** you replace the fitter with a learned γ-regressor. If you keep the physics fitter, ensembles of *runs* (multi-start optimisation from different initial guesses) are the physics analogue and are **cheap and immediately useful** — the spread across multi-start solutions directly detects wrong-branch landing, i.e. failure mode (e). [SYN] |
| **MC dropout** | [S] "casting dropout training in deep neural networks as approximate Bayesian inference in deep Gaussian processes ... gives us tools to model uncertainty with dropout NNs" ([Gal & Ghahramani, arXiv:1506.02142](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)). | **Not useful** unless you add a NN. | **Evidential deep learning** | [S] "we observe that our method achieves unprecedented success on detection of out-of-distribution queries and endurance against adversarial perturbations" ([Sensoy et al., arXiv:1806.01768](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01768)). | **Not useful for you now**, and treat the OOD claim sceptically: it is a classification method, its OOD performance is benchmark-dependent, and recent OOD benchmarks show that "none of them consistently outperforms the others" across shifted conditions ([arXiv:2501.18463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18463)). |

**[S] A caution that applies to all of the above:** Ovadia et al.'s large-scale benchmark found that "well calibrated uncertainty estimates convey information about when a model's output should (or should not) be trusted" but that **most methods degrade under dataset shift**, and only model-marginalising methods held up ([arXiv:1906.02530](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)). Since your *entire problem* is about shift, this is a strong argument for relying primarily on physics diagnostics.

### 3.4 Calibration of uncertainty — is your reported σ actually right?

This is the part most student teams skip and most judges reward. **[SYN] You cannot claim a 95 % interval unless you have measured its coverage.**

- **Reliability diagrams / coverage tests.** [S] Kuleshov, Fenner & Ermon: "Bayesian uncertainty estimates are often inaccurate — for example, **a 90% credible interval may not contain the true outcome 90% of the time**", and they propose a simple post-hoc calibration procedure that "is guaranteed to produce calibrated uncertainty estimates given enough data" ([arXiv:1807.00263](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00263)). **[S]** The recipe for you: (i) take M ≥ 100 repeated measurements on reference liquids with known γ; (ii) for each nominal level p ∈ {0.5, 0.68, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99} compute the empirical fraction of intervals containing the reference value; (iii) plot empirical vs nominal. A well-calibrated system lies on the diagonal. **This single plot is the most convincing validity evidence you can put in a report**, and it directly implements the ISO/GUM requirement that a confidence statement have a documented basis (§4.2).
- **Expected calibration error (ECE).** [S] Levi et al. show "the existing definition for calibration of a regression uncertainty [Kuleshov et al. 2018] has severe limitations in distinguishing informative from non-informative uncertainty predictions", propose a new definition, and give "an evaluation method using a simple histogram-based approach" that "clusters examples with similar uncertainty prediction and compares the prediction with the empirical uncertainty on these examples", plus "a simple, scaling-based calibration method [that] performs as well as much more complex ones" ([arXiv:1905.11659](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11659)). **[S]** Practical reading: **report ECE on binned uncertainty, and consider the scaling-based calibration — it is a one-parameter multiplicative correction to your reported uncertainty, i.e. nearly free.**
- **Tools.** [S] MAPIE has a dedicated Calibration module and examples ([MAPIE docs](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)); `netcal` (Apache-2.0) provides a "calibration framework" ([PyPI netcal](https://pypi.org/project/netcal/)); `uncertainty-toolbox` (MIT) provides calibration and sharpness metrics ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/uncertainty-toolbox/)). **[S]** MAPIE v1 also ships **exchangeability testing** ([MAPIE docs](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)) — use it to *justify* the claim that your calibration set is exchangeable with your operating conditions.

### 3.5 The key question: does ML-based OOD detection beat thresholding a physics diagnostic?

**Honest answer: there is no evidence in the tensiometry literature either way, and the general ML literature gives reasons to doubt that ML would win on your problem. Do not claim ML superiority, and do not claim physics superiority as established fact. Claim what you can defend: the physics diagnostic is interpretable, needs no OOD labels, and is available today.**

What the literature actually supports:

1. **[S] ML-for-pendant-drop-tensiometry already exists, and it uses physics quality indicators, not learned OOD scores.** Kratz & Kierfeld train a deep network on ~10⁶ numerically generated shapes; it is "four orders of magnitude faster" and, for **Wo < 0.1**, "gives on average a full order of magnitude more accurate estimates than CSF". And critically: their conclusion is that "ML gives a uniform absolute error over the full range of Wo numbers resulting in an exponent ν_ML ≈ −1. Therefore, **the Worthington number Wo is less indicative for the performance of the ML approach**" — while for conventional shape fitting Wo *is* the right indicator ([arXiv:2006.10111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10111)). **[S]** Two lessons: (i) a learned model *can* beat the classical fitter in the ill-conditioned regime — this is real and relevant to you; (ii) **the ML model's error is still governed by a physically-meaningful dimensionless group**, so your physics-based gate would still be the right gate even for an ML fitter.
2. **[S] General OOD benchmarks show that no ML method dominates.** OpenOOD, which implements 30+ methods, concludes "the field has progressed significantly over the past few years, where both preprocessing methods and the orthogonal post-hoc methods show strong potential" ([arXiv:2210.07242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07242)) — i.e. **simple post-hoc scores are competitive with elaborate ones**. And on harder, realistic shift benchmarks: "recent CLIP-based OOD detection methods struggle to varying degrees across the three proposed benchmarks, and **none of them consistently outperforms the others**" ([arXiv:2501.18463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18463)). **[S]**
3. **[S] Supervision matters more than method choice, and you have no OOD labels.** ADBench, "the most comprehensive anomaly detection benchmark with 30 algorithms on 57 benchmark datasets ... 98,436 [experiments] in total", is explicitly framed around "how do they perform with regard to (i) **varying levels of supervision**, (ii) different types of anomalies, and (iii) noisy and corrupted data?" ([arXiv:2206.09426](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09426)). **[S]** The consistent empirical finding across the anomaly-detection literature is that **semi-supervised methods (trained with some labelled anomalies) substantially outperform unsupervised ones, and unsupervised method rankings are unstable.** You have zero labelled OOD data. That is a decisive practical argument for the physics gate first. [SYN on the "decisive" framing; the supervision-matters finding is sourced to ADBench's framing.]
4. **[S] Even the classical benchmark datasets are too easy, which inflates ML OOD results.** For materials ML: "We evaluate generalization performance in over 700 out-of-distribution tasks that features new chemistry or structural symmetry not present in the training data. **Surprisingly, good performance is found in most tasks and across various ML models including simple boosted trees.** Analysis of the materials representation space reveals that **most tasks contain test data that lie in regions well covered by training data**, while poorly-performing tasks contain mainly test data outside the training domain. ... **most heuristically-defined out-of-distribution tests are not genuinely difficult and evaluate only the ability to interpolate.**" ([arXiv:2406.06489](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06489), published *Commun Mater* 6, 9 (2025).) **[S]** Directly transferable warning: if you build an "OOD test set" of, say, tilted drops, and your detector achieves 0.98 AUROC, check whether it is actually detecting *tilt* or merely detecting *larger residuals* — the latter is the physics diagnostic you were trying to beat.
5. **[S] There is a formal learnability limit.** "We find a necessary condition for the learnability of OOD detection. Then, using this condition, we prove several **impossibility theorems** for the learnability of OOD detection under some scenarios." ([Fang et al., arXiv:2404.04865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04865), JMLR 2024, journal extension of the NeurIPS 2022 Outstanding Paper "Is Out-of-distribution Detection Learnable?") **[S]** This is a rigorous reason why "add an OOD detector" is not a well-posed cure-all.

**Bottom line [SYN, clearly mine]:** For a pipeline with a *correct, differentiable, physically-derived forward model*, the residual and the conditioning numbers are **sufficient statistics for the failure modes you care about in a way that no distributional distance over image features is**. A density model over features can only tell you "this looks unlike my library", which is neither necessary nor sufficient for "my answer is wrong". A physics residual can tell you "the model that I am about to invert does not describe this observation". Use the physics gate as the primary and the ML as a calibrated-uncertainty layer on top.

## 4. Reporting and policy

### 4.1 What to report when a measurement is rejected

**[SYN — this is engineering judgement informed by GUM/VIM practice, not a published software standard. I found no standard that prescribes rejection reporting for measurement software.]**

Best practice, in decreasing order of importance:

1. **Never report a bare number.** Report a structured result object: `{value, uncertainty, coverage_factor, status, error_codes[], diagnostics{}, provenance{}}`. When rejected, `value = None` (JSON null), **not** `0`, **not** `NaN` as a float that downstream code will silently average.
2. **Report a machine-readable status enum** with named codes, one per gate. E.g. `OK`, `OK_MARGINAL` (reported with an explicit caveat), `REJECT_NO_DROP`, `REJECT_SEGMENTATION`, `REJECT_NOT_AXISYMMETRIC`, `REJECT_NOT_STATIC`, `REJECT_ILL_CONDITIONED`, `REJECT_NON_CONVERGENT`, `REJECT_MULTIPLE_BRANCHES`, `REJECT_UNCERTAINTY_TOO_LARGE`, `REJECT_OUT_OF_CALIBRATION_RANGE`.
3. **Report *why*, with the numbers.** "REJECT_ILL_CONDITIONED: Wo = 0.043, predicted relative error 16.7 % (Kratz–Kierfeld scaling), exceeds tolerance 5 %." This converts a refusal from a failure into a *diagnostic* and is what makes the system credible.
4. **Report the diagnostics even on acceptance.** This is the difference between "we think it's fine" and "here is the evidence". Include residual amplitude, asymmetry amplitude, χ²_red, Wo, P_s/Ne, predicted vs reported uncertainty.
5. **Support partial reporting.** GUM practice supports reporting a result with a larger stated uncertainty rather than refusing, *provided the uncertainty is honest* ([S] NIST TN 1297 §7: when reporting `U` or `u_c`, "if it is known that the interval which U = 2u_c or u_c defines has a level of confidence that differs significantly from 95 percent or 68 percent, it should be so stated as an aid to the users of the measurement result" — [NIST TN 1297 §7.4](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-7-reporting-uncertainty)). **[S]** So: a third state, **`OK_MARGINAL` / "report with expanded uncertainty and an explicit caveat"**, is legitimate and often more useful than a hard refusal. [SYN on the three-state design.]
6. **Make refusal traceable.** Log the image, the fitted parameters, the diagnostic values and the code. Rejections are your future training set — every rejection is a labelled example you didn't have before. [SYN]
7. **Report the *operating window*, not just the verdict.** State the validated domain (e.g. "validated for 0.5 ≤ Wo < 1.0, P_s ≥ P_s,crit(Bo), static axisymmetric pendant drops, 20–80 mN/m"). This is precisely the OECD "defined domain of applicability" principle translated to your instrument ([Sahigara et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/)). **[S on the principle.]**

### 5.2 Specific thresholds where the literature supports them

**Supported by source (use these):**
- **`Wo ≥ 1/2`** — the shape-diagram bifurcation boundary; below it, multiple shape branches exist. [S] ([arXiv:2006.10111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10111).)
- **`0 < Wo < 1`** — bounded; `Wo ≈ 1` is near-detachment. [S] (ibid.)
- **Predicted relative error `≈ 0.72 · Wo⁻¹`** (conventional shape fitting; `ν_CSF = −1.00, a_CSF = −0.72`). Convert to a refusal rule: refuse if `0.72/Wo > tolerance`. Note `a` is a fitted coefficient on mean relative error in Δρ̃; **treat it as a scaling law to re-fit on your own data, not as an absolute prediction.** [S on the coefficients, SYN on treating it as a re-fittable prior.]
- **Leverage warning threshold `h* = 3p/n`.** [S] ([Sahigara et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/).)
- **Distance-based AD threshold = 95th percentile of training distances** (converged with the `2d̄` rule in the comparative study). [S] (ibid.)
- **ADAN reliability threshold = 95th percentile per similarity criterion; reliability = count of unmet criteria out of 6.** [S] ([Carrió et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821140/).)
- **ADAN's traffic-light mapping (0–6 unmet criteria → 7 reliability classes → 3 colours)** as a reporting schema. [S] (ibid.)
- **χ²_red expected value 1**, ν = N − p. [SYN — textbook; I could not source a page during this research. Verify against any regression text.]

**Not supported — must be calibrated by you [SYN]:**
- Any specific numerical threshold on χ²_red, RMS residual in pixels, or asymmetry amplitude.
- Any Bond-number floor.
- Any conformal interval half-width tolerance τ (this should be set by your application requirement, not by literature).

**How to calibrate σ (the parameter everything depends on)** [SYN]:
1. Acquire ≥ 30 images of a **static pendant drop of a pure liquid with a well-known γ** (water at a controlled temperature is the standard choice) under your normal illumination and optics.
2. For each, fit and collect residuals. Pool the residuals **normalised by the local edge gradient magnitude**.
3. σ(z, x) ≈ (residual RMS) is your per-point edge-detection uncertainty model. If it is roughly constant, use a single σ.
4. **Validate χ²_red ≈ 1 on this set.** If χ²_red ≫ 1 with a correct model and a good drop, your σ is too small (i.e. you have unmodelled systematic error) — and this is itself the most important thing you can learn, because it means your *stated uncertainties are optimistic*.
5. Freeze σ. From then on, χ²_red ≫ 1 on a new image is evidence of a real problem.

### 5.3 What to do about the dynamic (rolling/sliding) extension

**[SYN, with one strong source.]** Your OOD design must be *different* for the dynamic case, because the physics changes, not just the input distribution. **[S]** For interfaces with adsorbed layers, "traditional shape-fitting techniques — which assume an isotropic and spatially homogeneous stress — are inapplicable for complex interfaces" and applying ADSA to them "lead[s] to incorrect predictions of the interfacial properties"; the paper also shows that "low Wo and low Ar sub-hemispherical geometries **do not guarantee** the existence of a homogeneous deformation or stress field at the interface, contrary to what is typically assumed in the CPT method", and that "**whether the assumption of a homogeneous dilatational deformation and constant interfacial stress ... introduces significant errors**" is assessed quantitatively, with noise sensitivity differing sharply between methods ([Rodríguez-Hakim, Jaensson & Vermant, *Rheologica Acta* 64:461–481, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00397-025-01493-z), open access CC BY). **[S]** Concrete implications: (i) a **static-fit validity gate is not transferable to dynamic data** — you need a separate calibrated gate; (ii) the relevant dimensionless groups are **Wo and an aspect ratio Ar = V₀/R_n³**; (iii) noise sensitivity is strongly method-dependent (they find CPT "fairly insensitive to noise" while "substantial errors in K arise when SFE is applied to noisy interface profiles, particularly for low–Ar configurations"), so **your noise-to-signal gate must be calibrated per method**. [S]

## 8. Executive summary

1. **Rename the feature.** This is an **applicability-domain / validity-domain** problem (QSAR, chemometrics, OECD principle 3) with an OOD component, not an OOD problem with a physics component. Start from [Jaworska et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16268757/) and [Sahigara et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6268288/).
2. **Build five gates, not one score.** (a) novelty/AD gate, (b) segmentation-quality gate, (c) axisymmetry/staticity gate, (d) conditioning gate, (e) convergence/branch gate. Report the count of failed gates as a reliability class, ADAN-style ([Carrió et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821140/)).
3. **The physics gives you two hard, published anchors:** the **Worthington number** with the power-law error scaling `MRE ∝ Wo⁻¹` (`a_CSF = −0.72, ν_CSF = −1.00`) and the **bifurcation boundary at Wo = 1/2** below which multiple shape branches exist ([Kratz & Kierfeld 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10111)). The classical **Bond number fails at small volumes** — use **Wo** or the **Neumann number** ([Yang et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28793193/)).
4. **Get Saad et al. 2010** ([PMID 20707340](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20707340/)) for the critical shape-parameter curve, and **Berry et al. 2015** for the field's canonical accuracy analysis. Both are paywalled; both are worth the effort.
5. **Calibrate σ first, then χ²_red becomes a real test.** Everything residual-based depends on an honest per-point edge-detection uncertainty. Validate that χ²_red ≈ 1 on reference liquids.
6. **Conformal prediction is the right way to make the uncertainty statement**, and interval width is a legitimate refusal criterion — but its guarantee needs exchangeability, which your dynamic extension will violate. Use Mondrian bins on Wo, and test exchangeability ([MAPIE](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)).
7. **Do not claim ML beats physics-based diagnostics, and do not claim the reverse.** No study compares them for this class of pipeline. Claim what is defensible: the physics gate is interpretable, needs no OOD labels, and is implementable today; the ML layer's job is calibrated uncertainty, not validity.
8. **Report refusals properly**: structured result, named error codes, the diagnostic numbers, a three-state verdict (accept / marginal-with-caveat / refuse), and a published operating window. Align the uncertainty statement with [NIST TN 1297 §7](https://www.nist.gov/pml/nist-technical-note-1297/nist-tn-1297-7-reporting-uncertainty) and the ISO/IEC 17025 idea of a validated method scope.
9. **Everything in the recommended stack is permissively licensed**: MAPIE (BSD-3), scikit-learn (BSD-3), PyOD (BSD-2), mlchemad (MIT), uncertainty-toolbox (MIT), netcal (Apache-2.0). **Avoid alibi-detect (Business Source License).**
